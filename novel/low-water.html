Low Water

A novel of Point Roberts, after Erskine Childers

What this is

An original novel set on the peninsula, built on the skeleton of The Riddle of the Sands (Erskine Childers, 1903), which is in the public domain. Childers' engine is borrowed and nothing else: two people, a small boat, and a thing that can only be found by reading a tide table correctly. The characters, the events and every word here are new.

It is written to be staged. Every scene below names a real place on the peninsula, an hour, and a state of the tide, so the model can put the light and the water where the prose says they are. February throughout: sunrise near eight, dark before five, and the border crossing at its emptiest — 35,796 vehicles that month against 59,425 in July, which is the figure the page already carries.

The engine

The water boundary between two countries runs offshore, across a tidal flat that dries for six hundred metres on a big ebb. Twice a month, on the lowest water of the series, there is a firm bottom from one side of the line to the other, and for about ninety minutes a thing can be walked or dragged across it where there is no road, no gate and nothing watching. The tide table says when. That is the whole of it, and it is what Wren has worked out and cannot get anybody to hear.

The mechanism is real. The flats off Lighthouse Marine Park and along the west shore dry substantially at low water, and the model already floods and exposes them from the live NOAA reading, so a scene set at 0.3 m MLLW will show the ground the prose walks on.

One

The border station · 16:10 · February

The lane was empty ahead of her and empty behind and she sat in it for eleven minutes with the engine off because the officer had taken her passport into the building and not come back. The light was going. It went early here and it went all at once, the way it does at forty-nine degrees in the second week of February, and by the time he came out the flag on the pole had gone from red to grey and the trees behind the canopy were one flat shape.

He handed the passport back through the window and asked her again what she was measuring.

The bottom, she said. The seabed. West of the point and round the south of it. There was a barge aground in November on a chart that said there was four metres of water, and somebody has to go and find out which of those two things is a lie.

And the equipment in the back.

A multibeam head, a motion sensor, two laptops and a tripod. It is on the manifest. It has been on the manifest since Tuesday.

He looked at the manifest for a while longer than reading it takes. Behind him the booth stood with its light on and nobody in it and the second lane coned off. She understood, sitting there, that the wait was not about her. There was nothing else to do here in February and she was the only thing that had come down the road in an hour.

Welcome to Point Roberts, he said, and gave her back the folder, and lifted the arm.

The road ran south into the dark under a line of firs and she followed it with her headlights on and the heater going. There were houses set back among the trees with no lights in them. A gas station with the pumps lit and the shop shut. A sign for a marina. Somewhere off to her right, past the trees, was the water she had come to measure, and she could not see it, but she could feel the road tilting down toward it the whole way.

Five square miles hanging off the bottom of Canada. She had read the file on the ferry. A surveying accident of 1846: a line drawn along the forty-ninth parallel by men who had not looked closely enough at what was below it, and this left over, a piece of one country you can only drive to through another. Twelve hundred people. One school. Three hours to the county seat by road, and two of those hours in a foreign state.

The motel was shut for the season. The woman who answered the number on the door said to try the marina, there was a place there that did rooms, and in any case that was where the boats were, and if she was here about the water then the boats were what she wanted.

She found the marina by driving until the road stopped. The basin was black and full of masts and the masts were moving, all of them, a little, out of time with each other, and the sound of it — halyards on aluminium, three hundred of them — came across the parking lot like something alive and unhappy. One light burned at the end of D dock. She sat with her hands on the wheel and watched it and it did not go out.

Nothing about the file had said February. The file had said fourteen days and a deliverable, and a barge and a judge and an insurer, and it had not said that the place she was coming to would be shut, or that the only water deep enough to work in would be water that emptied itself twice a day and left six hundred metres of ground behind, or that the ground it left would turn out to be the whole of it.

She got out of the car. The cold off the basin came at her in one piece. Down at the end of D dock the light was on in a sloop with a canvas dodger gone green at the seams, and a man was sitting in the cockpit in the dark in a survival suit with a mug of something, doing nothing at all, in February, at five in the afternoon, looking west at where the water would be when it came back.

What is written and what is not

Written: the treatment above and chapter one. That is roughly 1,400 words of a book that wants eighty thousand.

Borrowed: from Childers, the shape only — a summoned outsider, a boatman who has seen something, and a discovery that turns on the tide. The Riddle of the Sands is out of copyright and no sentence of it is here.

Invented: every person. There is no Ines Harrow, no Dale Wren, no Corinne Ashby, no Aubrey Lee. Nobody in this is anybody in Point Roberts, and the marina office keeps its published hours because that is the setting, not because the person in it is real.

Real: the geography, the 1846 boundary, the one road out through Canada, the flats that dry, February's light and February's traffic. The tide states named in the table are states this coast actually reaches.
